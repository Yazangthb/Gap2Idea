\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Gap2Idea builds on research into scientific discourse classification, the recognition of author-stated limitations and future work, and LLM-based analysis of scholarly documents. Across these areas, similar terminology can refer to different tasks: locating an existing statement, inferring a missing piece of knowledge, or generating a new criticism or research direction. We organise the discussion around these distinctions and explain how each line of work informs our funnel. To broaden coverage, we used LR-AI~\cite{alnakri2026lrai} for a supplementary structured search of OpenAlex and arXiv records; candidate papers retained here were checked against their primary records. Table~\ref{tab:related} compares the closest studies by target, method and input unit.

\subsection{Scientific discourse and sentence classification}

Scientific discourse annotation provides a foundation for identifying statements by their role within a paper. Argumentative zoning represents the rhetorical function of sentences and their relationship to the authors' argument~\cite{teufel2002summarizing}. Liakata et al.~\cite{liakata2010corpora} distinguish this rhetorical perspective from Core Scientific Concepts (CoreSC), which represent components of an investigation, including its goals, methods, observations and conclusions. The Dr.~Inventor corpus brings the discussion closer to our targets: its 40 computer-graphics articles have a Future Work category in the argumentative-structure layer and a Limitation category among the crosswise features~\cite{fisas2016multilayered}. These annotations establish a full-text precedent for representing both kinds of statement, although they do not constitute a single research-gap detection task.

Abstract sentence classification supplies further resources and modelling approaches. PubMed 200k RCT and CSAbstruct support the recognition of abstract roles in biomedical and computer-science publications, respectively~\cite{dernoncourt2017pubmed,cohan2019pretrained}. MAZEA allows multiple rhetorical labels per sentence~\cite{dayrell2012rhetorical}, while Emerald~110k extends coverage across disciplines and includes a combined Research limitations/implications heading~\cite{stead2019emerald}. Such resources provide useful discourse supervision, but their labels and document scope differ from ours: a conclusion sentence need not state a gap, and a combined limitations/implications label also covers other content.

More targeted rhetorical schemes come closer to our extraction targets. Sci-Sentence includes Research Gap and Limitation among seven categories intended to support literature-review analysis~\cite{bolanos2025scisentence}. Its benchmark samples sentences from introductions, related-work sections and limitations sections. Its Research Gap category overlaps with our open-problem target, whereas its Limitation definition concerns weaknesses of a previous study rather than exclusively the authors' own work. This difference in attribution matters when deciding which statements count as positives.

Context is another shared concern. Hierarchical sentence classifiers model surrounding discourse~\cite{jin2018hierarchical}, and Brack et al.~\cite{brack2024sequential} investigate cross-domain sequential classification through multi-task learning. Lan et al.~\cite{lan2024multilabel} extend sequential classification to LLMs and multi-label outputs, while Zhang and Zhang~\cite{zhang2024extracting} use context-enhanced representations to identify problem and method sentences. These studies motivate considering neighbouring text when a sentence is ambiguous. In Gap2Idea, local context is supplied to the final LLM filter, which decides whether a previously selected candidate expresses the target statement.

\subsection{Recognition of limitations and future work}

Work on self-acknowledged limitations directly addresses one of our three targets. Kilicoglu et al.~\cite{kilicoglu2018automatic} formulate limitation recognition in clinical research as sentence-level binary classification and investigate both rules and learned classifiers. Their use of section headings and limitation zones provides a direct precedent for structural localisation. Lan et al.~\cite{lan2024automatic} subsequently annotate limitations in 200 RCT publications and separate detecting a limitation from assigning its type. Their fine-tuned PubMedBERT detector achieves a reported $F_1$ of 0.821 under their evaluation protocol. SAL supplies our human-labelled limitation benchmark, and the PubMedBERT model family supplies a supervised comparison. We distinguish that published score from our full-paper evaluation because the evaluated sentence pool and class balance affect precision and $F_1$.

Future-work recognition has a parallel history. Hu and Wan~\cite{hu2015futureworks} use regular expressions to extract future-work statements, classify them into four categories and expose the results through a search prototype. Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu2019recognizing} use BERT-based recognition to identify future-research sentences in JASIST articles and cluster the extracted statements. The ACL FWS-RC corpus provides annotations and a taxonomy for future-work sentences in NLP papers~\cite{hao2020acl}. Using this resource, Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2023fws} recognise future-work sentences with feature-based classifiers, the strongest being Bernoulli naive Bayes (macro-$F_1$ of 0.9073), and classify their directions with neural models. Their recognition and direction-classification tasks are distinct, as are macro-$F_1$ and the positive-class $F_1$ used in our main evaluation. These published scores therefore provide context rather than a ranking against our paper-disjoint experiments.

Extracted statements also support analysis beyond detection. Suray et al.~\cite{suray2024future} examine how subsequent usable-security research engages with previously stated future work. More recently, Thaepprasit et al.~\cite{thaepprasit2026limits} analyse self-reported limitations in ACL and EMNLP papers through iterative human--AI qualitative coding. At the downstream ideation stage, Si et al.~\cite{si2025ideas} find in a controlled study with more than 100 NLP researchers that LLM-generated ideas were judged more novel but slightly less feasible than expert ideas. These studies show both the value of author-written statements and the need to evaluate extraction separately from the ideas produced from it. Gap2Idea addresses the preceding extraction step; it does not establish whether a stated gap remains unresolved or whether a proposed direction is useful.

\subsection{LLM extraction, inferred gaps and generated critique}

LLMs are increasingly used both to extract statements and to produce new assessments of research. BAGELS constructs limitation data from ACL, NeurIPS and PeerJ papers, supplements author-written material with external reviews, and studies retrieval-augmented limitation generation~\cite{alazher2025bagels}. FutureGen uses LLM-extracted author future-work statements, augmented with reviewer suggestions, as the reference for generating and evaluating new research directions~\cite{alazher2025futuregen}. Cheng et al.~\cite{cheng2026future} directly investigate prompted LLM extraction of future-work sentences from the full text of NLP papers and connect the extracted material to keyword-based research-direction analysis. Together, these studies establish that LLM extraction of author-stated gaps already has close precedents.

Knowledge-gap identification also includes an inference task. GAPMAP explicitly distinguishes stated biomedical knowledge gaps from missing knowledge inferred from context, evaluating LLMs in paragraph-level and full-paper settings~\cite{salem2025gapmap}. This distinction is central to our scope: we select an existing sentence that articulates a gap and preserve its wording. We do not ask the model to construct an implicit gap that has no corresponding author-written statement.

In limitation generation and scientific critique, the model writes the output rather than selecting existing sentences. LimGen studies suggestive limitation generation using 4068 ACL papers and their associated author-written limitations~\cite{faizullah2024limgen}. Despite its similar name, LimitGen is a separate benchmark for evaluating LLM-generated limitations and feedback on AI research papers~\cite{xu2025llms}. Tree-of-Concerns focuses specifically on unstated limitations through structured multi-agent critique~\cite{mishra2026tree}. These tasks can produce useful criticisms beyond those acknowledged by the authors. Our LLM instead returns a keep-or-drop decision for each candidate sentence; the resulting extraction remains attributable to the original text.

\subsection{Position of this work}

Our Stage~B builds on established methodology. Yin et al.~\cite{yin2019zeroshot} formulate zero-shot text classification as textual entailment, allowing a label to be expressed as a natural-language hypothesis. We apply this formulation to gap types using an existing NLI model. Here, zero-shot means that the classifier is not fitted on gap-detection training examples; the model has already been trained on NLI data, and our decision threshold is selected with labelled development data.

Ma et al.~\cite{ma2023reranker} provide the closest general information-extraction analogue: a small fine-tuned model filters examples and an LLM reranks the difficult subset. Their system routes by estimated difficulty, whereas our Stage~B selects possible gap statements and Stage~C makes a binary source-preserving decision. FrugalGPT~\cite{chen2023frugalgpt} places such staged designs in the broader family of LLM cascades that trade cost against quality. Our cascade differs by combining rules, a non-generative classifier and one LLM rather than routing queries among several LLM services.

Our contribution is the evaluation of this particular combination with three precision--recall instructions. We examine how the stages interact, how the filter settings behave across datasets, and how the funnel compares with giving the same LLM the whole paper, in accuracy and cost, for LLMs of different sizes. Long inputs are a known difficulty for LLMs~\cite{liu2024lost}; RULER likewise finds large performance drops with increasing context length across 17 models~\cite{hsieh2024ruler}. In long-context question answering, order-preserving retrieval can outperform full-context input while using fewer tokens~\cite{yu2024defense}. Closer to our setting, Wu et al.~\cite{wu2024lessismore} first extract key sentences from a long document and only then prompt an LLM to evaluate a summary, which lowers cost and raises agreement with human judgements. These tasks differ from sentence extraction, but they motivate testing whether candidate selection helps an LLM focus on sparse evidence. The evaluation covers both full papers and the section-level FWS resource, keeping their processing units and label sources explicit. Human-labelled limitation and future-work benchmarks support those two targets; findings for the joint three-type task remain provisional because that corpus is LLM-labelled. The resulting evidence concerns source-preserving extraction; assessing the quality of research ideas derived from these statements remains a separate task.
