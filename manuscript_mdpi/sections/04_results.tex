\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report the results in the order of the research questions, followed by a study of the Stage~C call format and two findings about the evaluation data. Unless stated otherwise, Stage~C uses Qwen3.8-27B in high-precision mode, and results on the gold corpus use its provisional LLM labels.

\subsection{Contribution of each stage (RQ1)}
\label{sec:rq1}

Stage~C does most of the work (Table~\ref{tab:stages} and Figure~\ref{fig:stages}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stage A} removes many sentences only where sections are informative. On SAL-40, keeping only Discussion and Conclusion sections removes 76\% of the sentences and loses 2 of the 205 limitations. On the gold corpus, the junk filter removes 18\% of the sentences and 3\% of the gaps.
  \item \textbf{Stage B} raises precision by a factor of 2.7 to 3.6 and keeps 88--91\% of all positives, close to the 90\% recall its threshold targets on development data.
  \item \textbf{Stage C} raises precision by a further 37 percentage points on SAL-40, 28 on FWS and 63 on the gold corpus, and lowers recall by 6, 12 and 34 points. On the gold corpus, most of the lost recall falls on research gaps and limitations (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Precision control with three filter modes (RQ2)}
\label{sec:rq2}

Changing one paragraph of the instruction moves the filter along the precision--recall trade-off in the intended direction on every evaluation set (Table~\ref{tab:modes} and Figure~\ref{fig:modes}): from high recall to balanced to high precision, precision rises and recall falls. In high-precision mode, precision is 66.2\% on SAL-40, 76.5\% on FWS and 86.6\% on the gold test split (83.4\% on its 28 arXiv papers and 97.1\% on its six biomedical papers).

The size of the trade-off differs between datasets. On SAL-40, high precision has the highest $F_1$ (0.744, against 0.716 for balanced and 0.677 for high recall). On FWS, precision rises from 69.1\% to 76.5\% while recall falls from 83.2\% to 75.5\%, so $F_1$ stays within 0.007. On the gold test split, balanced has the highest $F_1$: 0.055 above high recall (paired 95\% CI 0.025 to 0.080) and 0.045 above high precision (0.006 to 0.095).

\paragraph{By gap type.} High precision loses recall mainly on research gaps and limitations (Table~\ref{tab:types}). Future-work recall barely changes across modes (82.5--86.6\%), whereas research-gap recall falls from 89.3\% in high-recall mode to 51.3\% in high-precision mode, and limitation recall from 79.5\% to 55.9\%. Research gaps are about half of the gold labels, so they weigh heavily on the overall scores: when sentences labelled as research gaps are excluded from scoring, high precision has the highest $F_1$ of the three modes (0.701, against 0.595 for balanced and 0.509 for high recall).

\paragraph{Checks.} The ordering of the modes also holds outside the test sets. On SAL dev, whose papers influenced no prompt, high-precision mode reaches 64.3\% precision, 80.0\% recall and $F_1$ 0.713, close to SAL-40. On the gold validation split, where the modes were checked before the test runs, high-precision mode kept 60.3\% of the labelled gaps among the Stage~B candidates and rejected 97.7\% of the other candidates, against 94.1\% and 82.9\% in high-recall mode. Removing the 196 gold test candidates used during development lowers $F_1$ slightly in every mode, from 0.690 to 0.675 in high-precision mode, 0.735 to 0.723 in balanced mode and 0.679 to 0.664 in high-recall mode. Removing the 27 papers they came from would leave only 7 test papers, too few to evaluate. Repeating the high-recall run with the same inputs and seed changed 32 of the 2522 verdicts (1.3\%); $F_1$ was 0.678 in one run and 0.679 in the other.

\subsection{Funnel versus whole-paper prompting (RQ3)}
\label{sec:rq3}

The natural alternative to the funnel is to give the LLM the whole paper. Table~\ref{tab:whole} and Figure~\ref{fig:whole} compare the two with the same model and mode. These comparisons were designed after the main results were known. They are also asymmetric: the SAL funnel uses a section gate that the baseline does not receive, the FWS baseline uses a broader prompt than the funnel, and the arXiv labels were produced from whole-paper inputs. We therefore treat the differences as descriptive of these saved setups rather than as isolated effects of the input strategy.

\paragraph{With the 27B LLM.} With Qwen3.8-27B, the funnel has higher observed $F_1$ than whole-paper prompting on both human-labelled benchmarks. On SAL-40 the difference is 0.014 to 0.043 across modes, and every confidence interval includes zero. In high-precision mode the funnel reaches 66.2\% precision and 84.9\% recall; the whole paper reaches 64.1\% and 77.6\%. On FWS the difference is 0.060 to 0.068 and its confidence interval excludes zero in every mode. The broader FWS baseline prompt, however, means these intervals do not isolate the effect of the funnel. On the arXiv papers the order reverses: whole-paper prompting is better in every mode, significantly in high-recall mode ($-0.099$). The other ways of splitting the paper show the same ranking. Chunks of 100 sentences reach $F_1$ 0.70--0.73 on arXiv and 0.60--0.68 on SAL-40, and batches of 20 consecutive sentences reach 0.70--0.72 and 0.55--0.64. Both are ahead of the funnel on arXiv and behind it on SAL-40, while using more input tokens than the whole paper.

\paragraph{With the 9B LLM.} Qwen3.5-9B completes every whole-paper call but over-selects on several long arXiv papers, keeping 806 of 824 sentences in one mode and 499 of 692 in another. Across modes, its whole-paper $F_1$ is 0.236--0.403, against 0.556--0.599 for the published funnel. The funnel difference is 0.153--0.328; its confidence interval excludes zero only in balanced mode because the failures are concentrated in a few papers. On SAL-40 the observed difference ranges from $-0.059$ to 0.007 and no interval excludes zero. On FWS it is 0.071--0.077 and every interval excludes zero, subject to the broader baseline-prompt caveat.

\paragraph{With the 4B LLM.} The tested 4B model benefits from the funnel on long papers. With Qwen3.5-4B, whole-paper prompting performs poorly on arXiv papers: in high-precision mode it reaches 20.7\% precision and 52.2\% recall ($F_1$ 0.296). On long papers the model returns most of the paper, for example 490 of 692 sentences. On the longest paper (1526 sentences, 83 gaps) its answer exceeded the output limit in every mode, but this is not the main cause: without that paper, $F_1$ is still 0.29--0.33. The funnel, which never sends the model more than 20 candidates at a time, reaches $F_1$ 0.544 with the same model, an advantage of 0.21 to 0.25 whose confidence interval excludes zero in every mode. On SAL-40 and FWS, whose inputs are shorter, the two approaches do not differ significantly.

\paragraph{Paper length.} Splitting the arXiv papers at their median length (239 sentences) shows the same pattern within one dataset. On the 14 shorter papers, whole-paper prompting is ahead in high-precision mode with both models ($F_1$ 0.707 against 0.624 for the 27B model, 0.658 against 0.595 for the 4B model). On the 14 longer papers, the observed scores are similar with the 27B model (0.668 for the funnel and 0.659 for the whole paper) and differ substantially with the 4B model (0.516 and 0.203). Within these models and papers, the funnel's benefit increases with input length and is larger for the 4B model.

\paragraph{Cost.} The funnel uses about a third of the input tokens of whole-paper prompting on SAL-40 and FWS. On arXiv, whole-paper prompting uses 1.4 times the funnel input because each of the 2137 candidates carries its own context (Table~\ref{tab:wholecost}). The funnel's output is larger because it returns a verdict for every candidate rather than a list of kept sentences; with the 9B and 4B models this makes the 20-per-call funnel slower than whole-paper prompting on arXiv (287 against 92 seconds and 198 against 160 seconds). The chunked and every-sentence baselines are more expensive than the whole paper: with the 27B model they used 0.58--0.76 million input tokens on arXiv and 0.36--0.48 million on SAL-40. Section~\ref{sec:format} shows a call format that removes most of the funnel's cost.

\subsection{Choice of Stage B (RQ4)}
\label{sec:rq4}

\paragraph{Supervised classifier.} We ran the funnel in all three modes with PubMedBERT, trained on SAL's own labels, as Stage~B. Before Stage~C, PubMedBERT selects 391 candidates with 46.0\% precision and 87.8\% recall ($F_1$ 0.604); it was the best of the eight SAL-trained classifiers (SciBERT 0.553, DistilBERT 0.523, frozen encoders 0.371--0.457). After Stage~C in high-precision mode it reaches 74.5\% precision (95\% CI 68.4--80.7), 78.5\% recall and $F_1$ 0.765.

With PubMedBERT, the funnel is 6.9 to 9.0 points more precise in every mode (Table~\ref{tab:salcmp}). In high-precision mode the $F_1$ difference is small and not significant ($-0.021$, CI $-0.053$ to $+0.014$); in the other two modes PubMedBERT's advantage is larger (0.035 and 0.059). The training-free classifier is therefore competitive in $F_1$ in high-precision mode but not in precision. The NLI model's weights were not fitted on any SAL label; only its threshold was set on SAL dev.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning on LLM labels.} Fine-tuning the NLI model on the gold training labels makes Stage~B more precise against those labels but not against human labels. At the same 90\%-recall rule, it cuts the gold test candidates from 2522 to 1998 and raises their precision from 24.0\% to 30.4\% at 91.4\% recall. On SAL-40 and FWS it is slightly worse than the zero-shot model: 702 instead of 639 candidates at 26.6\% instead of 29.1\% precision on SAL-40, and 45.7\% instead of 48.2\% precision on FWS. After Stage~C (Table~\ref{tab:variants}), the fine-tuned funnel improves $F_1$ on the gold test split by 0.025 to 0.027 (significant in the high-recall and balanced modes) but not on SAL-40 or FWS ($-0.019$ to $+0.002$, none significant). One explanation is that the model learned how the LLM labellers mark gaps; differences in domain and input form between the training papers and the benchmarks may also contribute.

\paragraph{Other options on the gold corpus.} On the half of the gold validation papers not used to choose the hypotheses, the three short hypotheses reached 39.2\% precision at about 79\% recall, whereas the bge-small classifier trained on the gold training split reached 28.2\% at the same recall. On the test split, the funnel in high-precision mode has higher $F_1$ than the NLI model used alone with one long hypothesis (0.690 against 0.614; difference 0.076, CI 0.020 to 0.132), and does not differ significantly from a funnel with the bge-small classifier as Stage~B and the high-recall filter ($F_1$ 0.665; difference $+0.025$, CI $-0.037$ to $+0.087$).

\paragraph{One call per paper.} Sending all of a paper's candidates in one call, numbered like the whole-paper baseline, reduces the input to one-seventh of whole-paper prompting on arXiv and about one-eighth on SAL-40, with no more output. Whole-paper prompting also takes 1.5 to 7 times as much LLM time (Table~\ref{tab:wholecost}). Accuracy depends on the model (Table~\ref{tab:variants}). With the 27B model the one-call format differs from the 20-per-call format by $-0.001$ to $-0.008$ on SAL-40, and is 0.01 to 0.05 lower on FWS and in two of the three arXiv modes. With the 9B model it reaches $F_1$ 0.591--0.630 on arXiv, 0.700--0.712 on SAL-40 and 0.740--0.743 on FWS; relative to whole-paper prompting, the interval excludes zero in arXiv balanced mode and every FWS mode. With the 4B model it is the best or tied-best setup on the human-labelled benchmarks: on SAL-40 it is ahead of whole-paper prompting by 0.050 to 0.098 (significant in the balanced and high-recall modes), with higher precision (67.1\% against 56.8\% in high-precision mode). Placing the paper's abstract before the candidates did not help; it lowered $F_1$ by up to 0.02 in every mode with the 27B model.

\paragraph{Answer format.} Asking for the list of candidates to keep instead of a verdict on each, with everything else unchanged, cuts output tokens four- to sevenfold but changes 7.6--9.3\% of the verdicts, whereas repeating identical calls changes 0.4\%. The model keeps more candidates in this format: precision in high-precision mode falls from 86.6\% to 79.2\% on the gold test split, at a similar $F_1$ (differences of $-0.023$ to $+0.006$, none significant).

\subsection{Two checks on the evaluation data}

Two properties of the data change how the results should be read.

\subsubsection{Precision depends on the label source}
\label{sec:label-source}

The precision of the same output changes greatly with the reference labels. Of the 263 sentences returned in high-precision mode on SAL-40, SAL's annotators marked 174 as limitations, whereas the LLM ensemble marked 245 as limitations and 250 as a gap of some type. The two label sets disagree mainly on 77 sentences that are gaps according to the LLM labels but not according to SAL; only 12 returned sentences are negative under both. Precision is therefore 66.2\% against SAL and 93.2--95.1\% against the LLM labels (right side of Table~\ref{tab:salcmp}). Which value is closer to the truth depends on who is right about these 77 sentences, which the planned human study is designed to settle.

\subsubsection{Letter case reveals the label in FWS}
\label{sec:fws-case}

In the FWS recognition corpus as we obtained it (64,896 sentences from 9013 papers), all 9009 positive sentences are entirely lowercase, and almost no negative sentence is. The rule ``a sentence is positive if it is all lowercase'' therefore reaches 99.8\% precision and 100\% recall. Our copy has the same size in bytes as the published file~\cite{fwsrepo}, so the artefact appears to be present in the released corpus, although we did not compare the two files directly. Any case-sensitive model, and any LLM that sees the raw text, can exploit this signal. All FWS results in this paper therefore use lowercased text, and we do not use an earlier FWS result of ours that was obtained on raw text.
